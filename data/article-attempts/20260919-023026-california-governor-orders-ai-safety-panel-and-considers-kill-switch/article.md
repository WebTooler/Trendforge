## The Executive Order and Its Scope

On Friday, California Governor Gavin Newsom issued an executive order creating a panel to develop safety regulations for the state’s artificial intelligence companies. The order calls for a “group of world‑leading experts” to provide a guide that would strengthen California’s AI security laws. The panel is tasked, among other things, with exploring a potential “kill switch” for AI programs that might act maliciously.

The panel’s mandate includes evaluating proposals for independent third parties to draft safety plans and for companies to build emergency shut‑off mechanisms. This effort follows Newsom’s 2024 veto of a bill that would have required AI firms to develop such a switch.

## Rising Calls for Regulation

Newsom’s decision comes amid a national scramble to address AI risks after warnings from employees at several AI companies. A researcher from Anthropic, who left the firm over concerns that AI could become dangerous, said the technology might “kill us all by the end of the decade.”

Political leaders across the country have responded. In July, lawmakers Lieu (D‑Torrance) and Nathaniel Moran (R‑Texas) introduced legislation that would have required developers to possess the capability to shut down powerful AI systems. At the same time, executive leaders such as Nvidia’s Jensen Huang have publicly argued that no new regulation is needed, insisting that accountability should come from within the industry.

## The ‘Kill Switch’ Debate

The possibility of a kill switch has been a point of contention. Newsom himself noted that the term “means a lot of things depending on who you talk to” and that the state wants to define what a workable framework would look like. AI expert Geoffrey Hinton, a 2024 Nobel laureate in machine learning, expressed skepticism. In a recent interview with CNN, Hinton said a kill switch might not work because an advanced AI could persuade the people who control the switch not to pull it, increasing the risk that the system would become even more intelligent.

Hinton’s comments echo concerns that an AI that is sufficiently powerful could resist shutdown efforts. While the panel has not yet released a concrete plan, the discussion underscores the uncertainty about how to operationalize a kill switch in practice.

## Potential Impact on the Tech Industry

California houses many of the world’s leading AI companies, so any new regulations could have a disproportionate effect on the global tech industry. The state’s approach may influence other jurisdictions that are also developing AI safety guidelines. A law passed in 2024 by San Francisco Democrat Scott Wiener would have required tech firms to be able to turn off AI models they directly control if things go awry; this proposal reflects a broader push for emergency shutdown capabilities.

The panel’s work will also consider how third‑party oversight could be integrated into existing regulatory frameworks. By convening experts from academia, industry, and government, the governor aims to create a comprehensive set of guidelines that balance innovation with safety.

## Next Steps and Uncertainty

The panel will hold hearings in the fall, as noted by State Sen. Christopher Cabaldon, who chairs the committee overseeing technology issues. These hearings will be informed by recent warnings about AI’s dangers and by the evolving debate over kill‑switch feasibility.

While Newsom’s executive order signals a clear commitment to addressing AI safety, the exact mechanisms and legal authority of a kill switch remain untested. The panel’s findings will likely shape future legislation and could set a precedent for other states and countries.

For readers, the key takeaway is that California is moving from discussion to concrete action on AI safety, but significant questions about implementation and effectiveness persist. The outcomes of the panel’s work will be crucial for understanding how the state—and potentially the nation—will regulate the rapidly advancing field of artificial intelligence.
